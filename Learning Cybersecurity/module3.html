Level 1

Social Engineering & Phishing Defense 🎭🎣

Learn the tricks scammers use, spot fake messages and links, and check before you act.

🚪 Think of the School Gate

🧠 Real-life analogy: A stranger arrives at the school gate in a delivery uniform. "The head teacher sent me to collect the office keys," he says. "Quickly, she is waiting!"

The lock on the office is perfect. But if the guard hands over the keys, the lock does not matter.

The stranger did not break the lock. He tricked a person.

Social engineering means tricking people instead of breaking computers. Phishing is the most common kind. It is a fake message that pretends to come from someone you trust. It wants you to click a link, type a password, or send money or files.

  • Phishing usually arrives by email.
  • Smishing arrives by text message (SMS) or chat apps.
  • Vishing is a voice phone call.

🧠 The Tricks Scammers Use

Scammers use our feelings against us. When we feel rushed or scared, we stop thinking carefully. Learn these tricks so you can spot them.

Rush ⏱️

"Act in 20 minutes or your account will be deleted!" Hurry makes us skip checking.

Fake boss 👔

The message pretends to be from a head teacher, a bank or the police. We tend to obey people in charge.

Curiosity and bait 🎁

"See who looked at your profile!" Or a USB stick left in the corridor, labelled "Exam answers".

Kindness 🤝

"I'm new and I forgot my password. Can you log me in with yours?" Scammers use our wish to help.

Fear and threats 😨

"Pay now or you will lose your scholarship." Threats push people to act before thinking.

🎣 Worked Example 1: Spot the Warning Signs

Here is a made-up phishing email. Each 🚩 marks a warning sign.

From: "Greenfield IT Team" <it-help@greenfie1d-school.org>
                         🚩 1 ── "1" (one) instead of "l"
Subject: URGENT!! Account closing in 1 hour
                         🚩 2 ── rush
Dear Student,            🚩 3 ── no name, just "Student"

We found a problem with your account.
Click below or lose all your homework files.
                         🚩 4 ── fear
   [ Verify my account ]
   → really goes to http://198.51.100.88/login
                         🚩 5 ── link hides a different address
Type your password AND the 6-digit code
we just sent to your phone.
                         🚩 6 ── asks for password + MFA code
  1. Lookalike address. The real school is greenfield-school.org. Scammers swap letters that look alike, like 1 and l, or 0 and o.
  2. Rush. Real IT teams give you time.
  3. No name. Your school knows your name.
  4. Fear. A threat to make you panic.
  5. Hidden link. The button text and the real address do not match. On a computer, rest your mouse on a link (without clicking) to see where it goes.
  6. Asking for codes. No real school, company or bank asks for your password or MFA code.

💬 Worked Example 2: A Message From "Family"

Yusuf gets this chat message from an unknown number:

Salam Yusuf, it's Auntie Maryam. This is my new number.
I'm locked out of my account. A code is coming to your phone.
Please send it to me quickly! 🙏

Warning signs: a new number, a rush, and a request for a code. The code is probably a login code for Yusuf's account. The scammer is trying to take it over.

What Yusuf does: he does not reply with the code. He phones Auntie Maryam on her old number, the one already saved in his phone. She says she never sent the message. Yusuf blocks the number and reports it.

🔗 Worked Example 3: Who Really Owns This Link?

A web address can be built to fool you. The real owner is shown by the domain: the name just before the first single slash /. Read the domain from right to left.

https://greenfield-school.org/library
        └──── domain: greenfield-school.org ✅ real school

https://greenfield-school.org.login-check.example/library
        └──── domain ends in: login-check.example 🚩
              The school's name is just a decoration at the front.

The second link starts with the school's name. But it really belongs to login-check.example, someone else entirely.

⚠️ A padlock 🔒 in the address bar only means the connection is scrambled. It does not prove the site is honest. Many fake sites have a padlock too.

🕊️ Check Before You Act (Tabayyun)

The Qur'an teaches tabayyun: when news reaches you, check it before you act on it (Surah Al-Hujurat, 49:6). This is the best defense against phishing.

  1. Stop. Do not click, reply or pay yet.
  2. Do not use the contact details in the message. They may belong to the scammer.
  3. Check the request using a different way. For example, phone the person on a number you already know. Or type the website address yourself.
  4. Ask a trusted adult if you are not sure.

Experts call step 3 out-of-band verification. "Out of band" just means "through a different channel".

✅ What To Do: Your Anti-Phishing Checklist

  • ☐ Slow down when a message makes you feel rushed, scared or very excited.
  • ☐ Check the sender's address and the link's real domain.
  • ☐ Never share passwords or MFA codes. Nobody real will ask.
  • ☐ Never plug in a USB stick you found. Hand it to a teacher.
  • ☐ Check strange requests through a different way, such as a known phone number.
  • ☐ If you already clicked: do not panic. Tell a trusted adult or IT at once. Change your password from a safe device. Turn on MFA.

🟢 Level 1: Recall

Name three warning signs of a phishing message. What is phishing called when it comes by text message?

Show answer
Any three of: rush, a lookalike sender address, no name, threats, a link that goes somewhere different, a request for a password or code. By text message it is called smishing.

🟡 Level 2: Apply

After a flood, this message arrives. Find at least three warning signs, and say what you would do instead.

From: Flood Relief Fund <donate@reIief-help.example>
Only 2 hours left! Families need you NOW.
Send your gift card codes to this address.
Do not tell your parents, it's a surprise for them.
Show answer
  • Lookalike address: "reIief" uses a capital I instead of l.
  • Rush: "only 2 hours left".
  • Gift card codes: real charities do not ask for them.
  • Secrecy: "do not tell your parents" is a big warning sign.

Instead: tell a parent. Give through a charity you know, by typing its real website address yourself.
