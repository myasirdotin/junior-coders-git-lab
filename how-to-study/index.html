How to study

Learning is a skill, and like coding it gets better with practice. This page shows the ladder of thinking every lesson climbs, a tried way to learn programming, six habits that research shows really work, and how to plan your week.

The ladder of thinking (Bloom's taxonomy)

In 1956 the educator Benjamin Bloom and his colleagues sorted the kinds of thinking that learning asks for. In 2001 Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl updated the list into the six levels most teachers use today. Think of them as a ladder: each step needs the ones below it. For every new topic you climb it again, and you don't have to finish one step completely before trying the next.

Here is one topic, a for loop, at all six levels.

  1. 1

    Remember

    Recall facts and words.

    Example: What three parts go inside the brackets of a for loop?

    On this site: Level 1: Recall in every lesson, and the cheat sheets.

  2. 2

    Understand

    Explain it in your own words.

    Example: Say what this loop prints, and why it stops.

    On this site: "Predict the Output" in the textbooks and the exercise pages.

  3. 3

    Apply

    Use it in a new situation.

    Example: Write a loop that prints the shelf numbers 1 to 10 for the class library.

    On this site: Level 2: Apply, and the "Try It" part of every textbook chapter.

  4. 4

    Analyse

    Take it apart and find the cause.

    Example: This loop never stops. Find the line that causes it.

    On this site: "Code Detective" in the textbooks and the find-the-bug tasks on the exercise pages.

  5. 5

    Evaluate

    Judge, compare and choose, giving reasons.

    Example: Two classmates wrote loops to add up the charity donations. Which is clearer, and why?

    On this site: the marking rubric on the Projects page; use it on your own work and a friend's.

  6. 6

    Create

    Make something new of your own.

    Example: Build a page that lists every book in the class library and counts how many are borrowed.

    On this site: Level 3: Challenge, the Practice Arena and the projects.

Our three practice levels follow the same ladder: Level 1 (Recall) is steps 1 and 2, Level 2 (Apply) is steps 3 and 4, and Level 3 (Challenge) is steps 5 and 6.

Learning to program: PRIMM

Reading code comes before writing it, just as children read before they write stories. PRIMM is a way of learning programming that Sue Sentance and her colleagues worked out with school computing classes. It has five steps.

  1. Predict. Read a working program and write down what you think it will do, before you run it.
  2. Run. Run it. Were you right? If not, what surprised you?
  3. Investigate. Trace it line by line, answer questions about it, change one thing and see what happens.
  4. Modify. Change the program so it does something a little different.
  5. Make. Write a new program of your own that uses the same idea.

A shorter version of the same idea is Use, Modify, Create: first use someone else's program, then change it, then build your own.

Six habits that work

Scientists who study memory have tested many ways of studying. These six come out well again and again.

1. Test yourself

Close the book and write down what you remember, or answer questions without looking. Pulling an idea out of your memory makes it stronger than reading it again. This is called retrieval practice.

Try it: the quizzes and Level 1 tasks, before you look at the answer.

2. Space it out

Four short sessions over a week beat one long session the night before. Come back to a topic after one day, then after a few days, then after a week. This is spaced practice.

Try it: open a lesson you finished last week and do its quiz again.

3. Mix your practice

Once you know a few topics, mix them: a loop task, then an if task, then a function. You learn to choose the right tool, not just repeat the last one. This is interleaving.

Try it: on an exercise page, do one task from each lesson instead of all the tasks of one.

4. Study a worked example first

When something is new, read a solved example carefully before solving one yourself. Then try a similar task with the example hidden.

Try it: every textbook topic has an example with its exact output.

5. Explain it out loud

Explain your code, line by line, to a friend, a family member or even a rubber duck on your desk. Programmers call this rubber duck debugging: you often find the mistake while explaining.

Try it: after a lesson, explain it to someone in two minutes.

6. Type it, don't copy it

Type examples yourself instead of copying and pasting. Your fingers and eyes notice details such as brackets and semicolons, and your mistakes teach you how errors look.

Try it: in the Web Studio, retype an example from memory.

Habits that let you down

Some ways of studying feel productive but do little. Knowing them saves you time.

  • Reading the same page again and again. It feels familiar, so you think you know it. Test yourself instead.
  • Highlighting most of the page. Colour is not memory. Write a question for each important line and answer it later.
  • "I am a visual (or listening) learner." Careful studies have not found that teaching to a "learning style" helps. Everyone learns better with pictures and words together, and with practice.
  • Pasting code you don't understand, from a website or an AI tool. It may run, but you have learned nothing, and you can't fix it when it breaks. If you use borrowed code, read it until you can explain every line, and say where it came from.
  • Cramming the night before. You may pass tomorrow and forget next week. Space it out.
  • Studying with your phone beside you. Every message pulls your attention away. Put it in another room for the session.

A week of study

Here is one way to learn one lesson a week in four short sessions of 30 to 40 minutes. Change the days to fit your timetable.

WhenWhat to doHabit
Day 1Open the new lesson: Learn, See and Try. At the end, close it and write three things you remember.Worked example, test yourself
Day 2Do the Level 1 and Level 2 tasks without looking back. Check, then fix what was wrong.Test yourself, type it
Day 4Do the quiz from memory. Try the Level 3 challenge.Space it out
Day 6 or 7Mix tasks from this lesson and the last two. Explain the week's topic to someone. Work on your project.Mix, explain

Before every session

  • Phone in another room.
  • One clear goal, for example "finish the Level 2 tasks of lesson 4".
  • Two minutes of recall first: what did I learn last time?
  • Stop with a note of where to start next time.

The manners of a student of knowledge

"My Lord, increase me in knowledge."

The Qur'an, Sūrah Ṭā Hā 20:114

Muslim scholars have always taught that how you seek knowledge matters as much as what you learn. These manners (adab) make a better student of any subject, coding included.

  • A good intention (niyyah). Learn to benefit people and to please Allah, not to show off.
  • Patience (ṣabr). Code will break, often. Each error message is a lesson; stay calm and read it.
  • Regular revision. Those who memorise the Qur'an revise every day so it stays with them. Knowledge you don't revise slips away; that is spaced practice.
  • Asking, and respect for your teacher. Ask when you don't understand. Asking is not weakness; staying silent and confused is the loss.
  • Honesty (ṣidq). Hand in your own work, and say where borrowed code came from.
  • Acting on what you learn. Use it, teach it to someone else, and build things that help others ('ilm nāfi').
