# Chapter 3: Social Engineering — The Human Hack 🎭

## 🌟 Real-Life Story

A school spends a lot of money on a strong gate, card readers and cameras. One morning a smiling man arrives carrying two heavy boxes of library books. "Could you hold the door, please?" he asks. A kind student holds it open. Nobody checks who he is. All that technology was beaten by one polite request. That is **social engineering**.

**Social engineering** means tricking people, rather than computers, into giving away access or information. It works on feelings such as kindness, trust, fear and curiosity.

## 🎯 What You Will Learn

- Explain what social engineering is and why it works.
- Recognise five common methods: pretexting, baiting, phishing, tailgating and shoulder surfing.
- Use four questions to pause and check any unusual request.
- Respond safely when someone asks for a password or login code.

## 💡 The Trojan Horse: An Old Trick

An old Greek legend tells of the city of Troy. Its walls held out against an army for ten years. Then the army left a huge wooden horse outside the gates as a "gift". The people of Troy proudly wheeled it inside. At night, soldiers hidden in the horse climbed out and opened the gates.

The walls never failed. The **people** were tricked into opening them. Modern tricksters use the same human feelings:

- **Helpfulness and politeness:** "Could you just do me a quick favour?"
- **Trust in authority:** "This is the IT department."
- **Fear and urgency:** "Act now or your account will be deleted!"
- **Curiosity:** "You will not believe what is in this file."

None of these feelings is bad. Kindness and trust are good qualities. The defence is simply to **pause and check** before acting.

## 🎭 Five Common Methods

### Pretexting (a made-up story)
A **pretext** is a false reason given to hide the real one. The trickster invents a believable situation.

> "Hello, I'm Tariq from IT support. Someone just tried to log in to your account from another country. Please read me the six-digit code on your phone so I can lock them out."

Look at the tools used: **authority** ("IT support"), **fear** ("someone tried to log in") and **urgency** ("so I can lock them out"). Real IT staff never need your code. The code is what *they* would need to get in.

### Baiting (a tempting gift)
A USB stick labelled "Exam Answers 2026" is left in the school car park. Curiosity tempts someone to plug it in. The stick may hold **malware**: software built to cause harm (Chapter 11 covers it). Never plug in a found USB device. Hand it to a teacher or the office.

### Phishing, smishing and vishing (fake messages)
- **Phishing:** a fake email that pretends to come from a school, a delivery company or a charity.
- **Smishing:** the same trick by text message (SMS). For example: "Your parcel is waiting. Update your address at this link."
- **Vishing:** the same trick by voice call. Callers can fake the number shown on your screen. Some even use computer-made voices that sound like a real person.

Chapter 4 teaches you how to take a phishing message apart.

### Tailgating (following someone through a door)
A person without a pass follows closely behind someone who has one, often while carrying boxes so it feels rude to refuse. This is exactly what happened in our story.

### Shoulder surfing (looking over your shoulder)
Someone on a bus or in the library quietly watches you type your PIN or password. Cover the keypad, angle your screen, and be aware of who is near you.

## 🛡️ Defending the Human Firewall

A **firewall** is a barrier that blocks unwanted traffic. Aware people act as a "human firewall".

Islam teaches us to check news before acting on it (*Tabayyun*):

> *"O you who have believed, if there comes to you a disobedient one with information, investigate, lest you harm a people out of ignorance and become, over what you have done, regretful."* (Surah Al-Hujurat 49:6)

### The four questions to ask before acting
1. **Did I start this?** A message or call you did not expect deserves extra care.
2. **Is someone rushing me?** "Within 10 minutes or else" is a warning sign.
3. **Am I being asked for something secret?** A password, a login code, or a parent's card details.
4. **Can I check another way?** Hang up and call back using the official number from the school website or a printed letter. This is called checking **out-of-band**: through a separate channel the trickster does not control.

### Risky reply vs safe reply

| Situation | ❌ Risky reply | ✅ Safe reply |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Caller asks for your login code | "OK, it's 482 913." | "I never share codes. I'll call the school office myself." |
| Stranger asks you to hold the secure door | Holds it open | "Sorry, please use your own card or sign in at reception." |
| Message says "Your account closes today" | Clicks the link | Opens the site by typing its address, and checks there |

## 🏠 What to Do at Home

- Never share a password or a login code with anyone, including people claiming to be support staff.
- When a message makes you feel rushed or scared, stop. That feeling is the trick.
- Call back using a number you found yourself, not one the caller gave you.
- Do not plug in USB sticks you find.
- Tell a teacher or parent about strange requests. Reporting helps protect others too.

## ⚠️ Common Mistake

**Mistake:** "I would never fall for that. Only careless people get tricked."

**What happens:** tricksters target busy, tired or kind people at the worst moment, such as during exams or just before a deadline. Clever people are tricked every day because the message *feels* real.

**Fixed thinking:** "Anyone can be tricked, so I follow my checking routine every time." A habit protects you even when you are rushed.

## 📝 Recap

- Social engineering tricks people instead of computers.
- It uses good feelings, such as helpfulness and trust, and strong ones, such as fear and urgency.
- Five common methods: pretexting, baiting, phishing (and smishing, vishing), tailgating and shoulder surfing.
- Ask four questions: Did I start this? Am I rushed? Is a secret wanted? Can I check another way?
- Nobody genuine ever needs your password or login code.
