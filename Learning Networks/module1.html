The Internet Highway & Packets 🌐📦

Welcome to Computer Networking! Learn how physical light, electricity, and digital packets connect humanity across oceans.

The "Postcard Network" Analogy ✉️

🧠 Real-life analogy: Imagine sending a 40-page science report to a friend in another city, but the post office only accepts postcards. You copy one page onto each postcard. You write the address on every card. You also write "page 7 of 40" in the corner. The cards may travel on different trucks and arrive in a mixed-up order. Your friend uses the page numbers to put the report back together.

Computers do the same thing. They do not send a whole file or web page in one go. They cut the data into small pieces called packets.

Each packet carries three main things:

  • Addresses: who sent it and where it is going. (These are called IP addresses. You will study them in Module 3.)
  • A sequence number: a number that tells the receiver where this piece belongs, like "page 7 of 40". It lets the receiver put the pieces back in the right order.
  • The payload: the actual piece of your data. It is the message itself, not the address label around it.
✉️

The Postcard (Packet)

Carries the sender and receiver addresses, a sequence number and a small piece of your data (the payload).

🚦

The Switch & Router

A switch passes packets between devices in one building. A router passes packets from one network to the next, like a sorting office.

How Big Is One Packet? 📏

Every kind of link has a size limit for one piece. This limit is called the MTU (maximum transmission unit).

On a normal wired network (Ethernet), one frame can carry up to 1,500 bytes. That is its MTU. A byte is a group of 8 bits, enough to store one letter such as "A".

Those 1,500 bytes are not all your data. They also include the address labels: the IP header and the TCP header. A header is a small block of labels at the front of a packet. Together these two headers usually take about 40 bytes. So the payload in each packet is usually about 1,460 bytes.

You will meet each header properly in Modules 2 to 4.

Worked example 1: cutting a file into packets

Aisha uploads a 6,000-byte science report. Each packet can carry about 1,460 bytes of payload. How many packets does she need?

File: science-report.pdf = 6,000 bytes
Payload per packet       ≈ 1,460 bytes

Packet 1: bytes     1 – 1,460
Packet 2: bytes 1,461 – 2,920
Packet 3: bytes 2,921 – 4,380
Packet 4: bytes 4,381 – 5,840
Packet 5: bytes 5,841 – 6,000
          (only 160 bytes)

Answer: 5 packets

Four full packets carry 4 × 1,460 = 5,840 bytes. The last 160 bytes need a fifth packet. Real computers number the bytes, not the packets, but the idea is the same.

LAN and WAN: Near and Far Networks 🏫🌍

A LAN (local area network) is a small network in one place. A school computer lab is a LAN. So is your home Wi-Fi.

A WAN (wide area network) joins networks across cities and countries. The Internet is the biggest WAN of all. It is a network of networks.

Two kinds of boxes move packets:

  • A switch connects devices inside one LAN. It sends each packet only to the device it is meant for.
  • A router connects different networks. It reads the destination and chooses the next step on the path. Each step from one router to the next is called a hop.

Worked example 2: a packet's trip from school to a library website

[Laptop] ──cable──► [Switch]
                        │
                        ▼
              [School router]  ← LAN ends
                        │
                        ▼
                 [ISP router]  ← WAN begins
                        │
                        ▼
              ... more hops ...
                        │
                        ▼
              [Library server]

An ISP (Internet service provider) is the company that connects your school or home to the Internet. The packet crosses the LAN first, then many routers in the WAN. The reply comes back too, though it may take a different path.

The Four Physical Media of Networking

Packets must travel through something real. These are the four common paths:

Fiber-Optic Cables 💡

Flashes of light travel inside thin glass strands, even across ocean floors. Light in glass moves at about 200,000 km per second.

Copper Ethernet (Cat6) ⚡

Electrical pulses in copper wires. Used for short, fast links inside schools, offices and homes (up to about 100 metres per cable).

Wi-Fi & Radio Waves 📡

Radio waves carry data through the air. Wi-Fi uses 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz and, on newer routers, 6 GHz. GHz (gigahertz) means billions of waves per second.

Cellular (4G / 5G) 📱

Tall tower antennas give phones a connection across towns and along roads.

The 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi band reaches farther and passes through walls better. The 5 GHz and 6 GHz bands are usually faster but cover a smaller area.

Bandwidth vs. Latency: The Water Pipe 🚰

Bandwidth (width of the pipe): how much data can flow each second, for example 100 Mbps.
Latency (length of the pipe): how long one small message takes to arrive, for example 24 ms.

Mbps means megabits per second: millions of bits each second. A bit is a single 0 or 1. ms means milliseconds: thousandths of a second.

A wide pipe moves lots of water. But a long pipe still makes the first drop wait. That is why bandwidth and latency are different things.

Worked example 3: measuring latency with ping

The ping command sends a tiny "are you there?" message to another computer. The other computer sends a tiny reply. Ping measures the round-trip time: the time for the message to reach the other computer and come back. Most of that time is spent travelling through cables and waiting in routers along the way.

ping 1.1.1.1

Example output on Windows (your numbers will be different):

Pinging 1.1.1.1 with 32 bytes of data:
Reply from 1.1.1.1: bytes=32 time=14ms TTL=57
Reply from 1.1.1.1: bytes=32 time=13ms TTL=57
Reply from 1.1.1.1: bytes=32 time=16ms TTL=57
Reply from 1.1.1.1: bytes=32 time=14ms TTL=57

Ping statistics for 1.1.1.1:
    Packets: Sent = 4, Received = 4, Lost = 0 (0% loss),
Approximate round trip times in milli-seconds:
    Minimum = 13ms, Maximum = 16ms, Average = 14ms
  • time=14ms is the round-trip time for that one message.
  • Lost = 0 means every message came back. Lost messages are a sign of a weak or busy link.
  • TTL=57 is a hop counter. Each router lowers it by one, so a lost packet cannot travel in circles forever.

On macOS and Linux, ping keeps going until you press Ctrl + C. Add -c 4 to send only four messages.

Worked example 4: why distance matters

A science-museum server is 1,000 km away. Light in fiber covers about 200,000 km each second. What is the fastest possible round trip?

One way   : 1,000 ÷ 200,000 s = 0.005 s = 5 ms
Round trip: 5 ms × 2          = 10 ms

Real ping will be higher, because packets
also wait inside routers, and cables rarely
run in a straight line.

Common Misunderstanding ⚠️

"A faster Internet plan will make my ping lower."

Not usually. A bigger plan gives you more bandwidth (a wider pipe). Ping measures latency (how long the pipe is). Latency depends mostly on distance and on how long packets wait in routers. A wider pipe helps big downloads. It does not make a far-away server closer.

"Wi-Fi is the Internet."

Wi-Fi is only the short radio link between your device and your router. After the router, your packets travel through cables and many other routers.

🤝 Beneficial knowledge: These same packets carry online lessons to remote villages, test results between hospitals and messages between families far apart. Understanding them helps you build networks that serve people well.
